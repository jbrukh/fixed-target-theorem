# A dense-case theorem for Seymour's second neighborhood conjecture

A short proof that Seymour's second neighborhood conjecture holds for every
oriented graph whose order is small relative to its minimum outdegree.

Jake Brukhman (CoinFund).

## What the theorem states

Let $G$ be a finite oriented graph — a directed graph with no loops and no pair
of opposite arcs — on $n$ vertices, with minimum outdegree $\delta$. Write
$N_1(v)$ for the set of outneighbors of $v$, and $N_2(v)$ for the *exact* second
outneighborhood: the vertices reachable from $v$ in two steps but not in one. A
vertex $v$ is a **Seymour vertex** if

$$|N_2(v)| \ge |N_1(v)|.$$

> **Theorem (Dense-case theorem).** If $n = 2\delta + 2$, then $G$ has a
> Seymour vertex.

Together with Fisher's theorem for tournaments, this immediately gives the
following dense-case corollary: if $n \le 2\delta + 2$, then $G$ has a Seymour
vertex.

Seymour's second neighborhood conjecture asserts that *every* finite oriented
graph has a Seymour vertex. It remains open in general. Known cases include
tournaments (Fisher, 1996) and minimum outdegree at most six (Kaneko and Locke,
2001); a 2026 preprint of Sadhukhan, Sandeep, and Sen treats minimum outdegree
seven. For dense incomplete graphs, Fidler and Yuster proved the conjecture for
prescribed missing-edge structures including matchings, stars, and cliques,
Ghazal extended this direction to generalized stars, and Daamouch, Ghazal, and
Al-Mniny treated further prescribed structures such as two stars and disjoint
paths. The theorem here requires no prescribed structure on the missing edges.

Every oriented graph satisfies $n \ge 2\delta + 1$, with equality only for a
regular tournament. Thus Fisher handles the equality boundary, while the new
dense-case theorem handles the next order. Equivalently, any counterexample
to the conjecture must satisfy $n \ge 2\delta + 3$. Combined with the known
minimum-outdegree results, this raises the best known lower bound on the order
of a counterexample from 16 to 17 vertices — and, conditional on the 2026
preprint, from 18 to 19.

## How the proof works

The argument reverses the usual point of view. Instead of fixing a source vertex
$v$ and asking what it reaches, fix a **target** $x$ and collect the sources
that fail to reach $x$ in at most two steps. Each such source, together with its
entire outneighborhood, is confined to the *trap* $T(x)$ — the vertices sending
no arc to $x$. Counting arcs inside the trap bounds how many sources can miss
the same target (the capacity lemma). Double-counting unreachable source–target
pairs then sets the demand generated by minimum-outdegree vertices against the
total capacity of all traps, and for $n = 2\delta + 2$ the two are
incompatible.

The new fixed-target proof is purely combinatorial and self-contained: a few
pages, with no computer-assisted step and no connectivity or minimality
hypothesis.
